## Research question

**Do more complex landmark-derived features always produce more accurate and robust sign-language recognition?**

This study followed the emergency sign language interpretation application and evaluated the impact of feature selection and input perturbations rather than assuming that richer representations must perform better.

## Experimental overview

| Dimension | Landmark-derived feature representation |
|:--|:--|
| **153D** | Upper-body and both-hand landmark coordinates (51 landmarks × 3) |
| **306D** | Positions plus temporal displacement features |
| **317D** | Extended geometric/relational information |
| **347D** | Further hand/finger-related geometric information |

| Component | Investigated conditions |
|:--|:--|
| Recognition architectures | 1D-CNN, Bi-LSTM, Transformer |
| Input corruptions | Landmark noise, partial hand occlusion, frame skipping |
| Evaluation | Clean recognition and change in Macro-F1 under perturbation |
| Sequence representation | 70-frame landmark sequences |

## Main observation

The simplest **153D coordinate representation** was competitive and often the best under clean-input conditions. Adding motion or geometric features did **not** lead to a uniform increase in accuracy or robustness across models and perturbation types.

**Takeaway:** Feature richness is not synonymous with robustness. A suitable representation should be selected jointly with the recognition architecture and expected input quality.

## Why this leads to the next study

The CICS investigation focuses on sensitivity to degraded landmark observations. This motivates a more specific question for the subsequent missing-landmark analysis:

> When landmarks are absent and subsequently reconstructed, which errors remain in motion and geometric features—and how do they affect sign recognition?
